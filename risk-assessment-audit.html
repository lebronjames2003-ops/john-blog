Beyond the Risk Score: Revisiting Our Risk Assessment Through an Auditor’s Eyes

An auditor’s perspective on asset ownership, classification, control evidence, and the lessons from our group’s Risk Assessment Form.

By John • October 16, 2026 • 9-minute read
🔐

Know your assets.
Verify your controls.

Clear ownership and reliable evidence make risk treatments easier to trust.

When our group completed its Risk Assessment Form, we focused on identifying threats, rating their possible effects, and recommending practical treatments. Revisiting that form through an auditor’s eyes changed my understanding of what a convincing assessment requires. A completed table can show that an organization recognizes risks, but it cannot, by itself, prove that assets are fully accounted for or that controls work consistently.

Our assessment concerned a community healthcare organization. To protect confidentiality, this reflection uses general asset categories and role descriptions. It excludes names, locations, security arrangements, and details that could expose weaknesses. This is a review of our assessment document, not a completed independent audit. Where evidence is missing from the form, I cannot assume that the organization has no evidence elsewhere.

The central question I would now ask is: could the organization demonstrate that it knows what it holds, who is accountable, and how those assets are protected throughout their useful lives?

Recognizing Assets Is Only the Beginning

Our form identifies several important categories: patient records, archived records, medicines, vaccines, medical supplies, equipment, and facilities. This is a useful starting point because healthcare depends on physical resources and information. Our group also considered existing controls, additional treatments, responsible personnel, and effectiveness reviews rather than stopping at a list of threats.

However, broad categories do not establish the completeness of an inventory. “Medical equipment,” for example, does not tell an auditor how many items exist, which items support essential services, or whether every item has a maintenance history. Similarly, “patient records” does not establish the number of record collections, their formats, their retention requirements, or their movement between authorized users.

An auditor would reconcile an asset register with purchasing records, stock records, maintenance documents, and physical observations. Testing should work in both directions: select registered assets and locate them, then select items observed during a walkthrough and confirm that they appear in the register. This would help identify missing assets and unrecorded holdings.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 emphasizes maintaining asset inventories and prioritizing assets according to classification, criticality, and mission impact (Pascoe et al., 2024). Although our assessment extends beyond cybersecurity, I find that principle useful: protection decisions depend on knowing which resources enable the organization’s services.

I would also ask whether supporting resources fall within the assessment’s scope. Supplier services, staff capabilities, utilities, and any technology used to handle information may affect the assets already listed. Their absence from our form is a question for follow-up, not proof that the organization overlooked them in practice.

Which Assets Mattered Most?

Patient information mattered because it supports care and carries an expectation of privacy. Confidentiality concerns who may access it; integrity concerns whether it remains accurate and complete; availability concerns whether authorized personnel can retrieve it when needed. A record can be securely stored yet still create problems if staff cannot find it or determine whether it is current.

Medicines and vaccines mattered because their availability and condition directly support service delivery. Equipment mattered because reliable operation supports safe and timely care. Facilities and storage resources mattered because they protect several other assets at once. Archived records also deserved attention: a record’s age alone does not determine whether it has lost its sensitivity or usefulness.

I would therefore separate information sensitivity from operational criticality. A supply item may contain no confidential information but still be essential to a service. Conversely, a rarely accessed record may remain highly sensitive. The following is a proposed classification and ownership approach, rather than a description of an existing approved policy.

Proposed Asset Classifications and Accountable Owners
Asset Category Proposed Classification or Priority Proposed Accountable Owner
Patient and archived records Restricted information; availability requirements based on care and retention needs Designated records owner
Medicines, vaccines, and essential supplies Controlled stock; criticality based on service impact Designated clinical supply owner
Medical equipment Operational assets; criticality based on intended use and service dependency Designated equipment owner
Facilities and storage resources Supporting physical assets; priority based on the services and holdings protected Designated facilities owner
Inventory, maintenance, and audit records Internal information, with access restrictions where sensitive details appear Owner of the relevant management process

Management should approve these categories and define their handling requirements. Classification should guide access, storage, checking, retention, and disposal rather than simply adding another label to the register.

Responsibility Must Mean Accountability

Our form assigns treatments to staff roles and groups. That is helpful because it recognizes that someone must act. From an audit perspective, however, a shared description can leave uncertainty about who approves decisions, checks completion, or escalates an overdue action.

I would distinguish the asset owner from the custodian and control performer. The owner is accountable for protection requirements and risk decisions. A custodian handles the asset in daily work. A control performer completes a particular check, while a reviewer evaluates the result. In a small organization, one person may perform several roles, but management should still define those responsibilities and arrange proportionate oversight.

For example, the person who records stock movements need not be the sole reviewer of discrepancies. An equipment user may perform routine checks while the equipment owner ensures that maintenance is arranged and problems are resolved. Clear responsibilities would make our recommendations easier to implement and audit.

What Evidence Would Convince an Auditor?

The most important change in my thinking is that a control description is a claim requiring support. Saying that checks occur regularly does not establish that they occurred throughout the period under review or that problems were corrected.

NIST’s assessment guidance describes three methods: examining evidence, interviewing relevant personnel, and testing controls under defined conditions (Joint Task Force, 2022). I would adapt those methods to our assessment rather than treating the publication as a complete healthcare audit standard.

For inventory controls, I would examine dated stock records and reconcile a sample with physical quantities and transaction documents. For equipment, I would request asset identifiers, maintenance histories, completed inspection records, and evidence that defects were addressed. For information handling, I would review approved access responsibilities and observe an authorized retrieval-and-return process without copying identifiable patient information.

I would also distinguish control design, implementation, and operation. A written procedure may describe a sensible control. Observation may show that it has been introduced. Records covering several periods, together with appropriate testing, would help establish whether it operates consistently. A photograph or one completed checklist would provide limited support for a claim of sustained effectiveness.

Evidence should identify what was checked, when, by whom, and what happened when an exception arose. Interviews would help explain the process, but I would seek corroboration. Audit working papers should record the review period, sample selection, results, and limitations, with sensitive evidence retained securely rather than reproduced in a public blog.

Gaps an Auditor Would Likely Investigate

The first likely gap is traceability. Our form links risks to asset categories, but it does not provide a detailed inventory or references connecting individual assets to supporting evidence. Without those links, an auditor may struggle to establish whether a treatment covers the whole relevant population.

The second gap is the basis for effectiveness ratings. Our form includes lower ratings after proposed treatments, but the document does not establish a testing history supporting every reduction. Some remarks express expected improvement. I would label those ratings as projected until implementation and operating evidence justify a verified residual-risk assessment.

I would also challenge reductions in impact separately from reductions in likelihood. A preventive control can make an incident less likely while the consequences remain serious if it occurs. Any lower impact rating needs an explanation of how the treatment limits the resulting harm. A lower combined score should follow that reasoning rather than replace it.

The third gap is scheduling. Entries such as “ongoing” or “immediate” communicate intent but do not define measurable completion. A recurring control needs a frequency, a responsible role, a review point, and an escalation process. An improvement project needs a completion date and acceptance criteria.

The fourth gap concerns treatment consistency. Where a risk is accepted while additional improvements are still proposed, the decision needs clarification. Management should identify what is being accepted, who has authority to accept it, and when the decision must be reviewed. Recommendations and acceptance can coexist, but the record should explain their relationship.

Finally, some recommended activities resemble existing controls. Repeating an activity may be appropriate, but the treatment should explain what will improve: coverage, frequency, documentation, supervision, or exception handling. Otherwise, the revised assessment may suggest progress without showing a meaningful change in protection.

These are limitations and questions arising from our document. They are not confirmed findings that staff failed to perform their duties.

Making Our Treatments Stronger

An asset inventory would make our recommendations more precise. I would include an asset identifier or collection identifier, description, quantity where relevant, controlled location reference, classification, criticality, accountable owner, custodian, lifecycle status, related risk, and review date. Stock and equipment records would need additional fields suited to their purpose. The inventory itself would require controlled access and regular updating.

An audit checklist would then translate each treatment into a verifiable question. For each control, it should identify the criterion, required evidence, assessment method, sample, result, exception, corrective-action owner, and follow-up date. Instead of asking only whether a checking procedure exists, the checklist would ask whether sampled checks were completed and whether exceptions received appropriate action.

Together, these tools would strengthen the treatments our group recommended for stock monitoring, equipment care, information protection, and physical asset management. They would show what each treatment covers, who must sustain it, and what evidence supports its effectiveness. They would also help management distinguish completed improvements from actions still awaiting resources or verification.

Revisiting our form taught me that risk assessment continues after a score is assigned. I now see an asset inventory as the foundation for understanding what needs protection and an audit checklist as a way to test whether that protection is dependable. Our recommendations become more credible when they can be traced from an asset to an accountable owner, a defined control, and reliable evidence. That would make our assessment more useful to the organization and more defensible under independent review.
