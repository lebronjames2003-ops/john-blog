What Our Risk Assessment Fieldwork Taught Us About Information Security

A reflection on what our group learned from conducting a real risk assessment and how it helped us understand information security in practice.

By John • September 4, 2026 • 10-minute read
🔐

Understand the risk.
Protect what matters.

Information security is about people, information, resources, and everyday decisions.

Before doing our risk assessment fieldwork, I thought information security was mostly about computers, passwords, viruses, hackers, and protecting online accounts. Most of the examples we discussed in class were related to technology, so I assumed that security mainly depended on software and computer systems. However, after our group conducted an actual risk assessment in an organization, I realized that information security is much wider than that.

Our fieldwork taught us that information security also involves people, physical records, equipment, supplies, storage areas, work procedures, and even the way an organization prepares for problems. Security is not only about stopping hackers. It is also about protecting important information and resources from being lost, damaged, changed, accessed by the wrong person, or becoming unavailable when they are needed.

For confidentiality, I will not mention the name of the organization, the names of its workers, or any specific weakness that we observed. I will simply refer to it as a local community health service organization. I believe this is also part of being responsible when doing a risk assessment. The information gathered during an assessment may be sensitive, so it should not be shared publicly in a way that could harm the organization.

Seeing Information Security in Real Life

One of the biggest lessons I learned from this activity was that information security looks different in real life compared with how we sometimes imagine it in the classroom.

When we hear the words "information security," we usually think about computers, databases, passwords, and internet attacks. During our fieldwork, however, we saw that important information and resources can also exist outside a computer.

For example, organizations may use printed records, documents, equipment, medicines, supplies, storage areas, and other physical resources. If these things are lost, damaged, changed, misplaced, or accessed by unauthorized people, the organization can still experience a security problem.

This helped me understand the CIA triad more clearly.

The first part is confidentiality. This means making sure that private or sensitive information can only be accessed by people who are allowed to see it.

The second is integrity. This means making sure information is correct, complete, and not changed in an improper way.

The third is availability. This means making sure that information, equipment, and important resources are available when people need them.

ISO explains that information security management protects confidentiality, integrity, and availability through a risk management process (International Organization for Standardization [ISO], 2022).

During our fieldwork, these three ideas became easier for me to understand because we could connect them to actual situations instead of only memorizing their definitions.

Learning How to Identify Risks

Another important thing we learned was how to properly identify a risk.

At first, it was easy to simply point out something that looked unsafe and immediately call it a risk. But as we continued our assessment, we learned that we needed to think more carefully.

We had to ask questions such as: What asset could be affected? What could go wrong? Why could it happen? How serious would the result be? How likely is it to happen? What protection is already in place?

This made our assessment more organized.

For example, instead of only saying that something might be damaged, we needed to think about what could cause the damage, what part of the organization would be affected, and what the possible result would be.

This was important because not every problem has the same level of risk.

Some problems may happen often but have only a small effect. Other problems may be unlikely to happen but could cause a very serious effect if they occur.

Because of this, we learned that risk assessment should not be based only on feelings. We need to use reasonable information and judgment.

Understanding Impact and Probability

In our risk assessment form, we looked at impact and probability.

Impact refers to how serious the effect could be if a risk happens. Probability refers to how likely the risk is to happen.

We used these two factors to help determine the level of risk.

This part of the activity helped us understand why organizations cannot treat every risk as equally important.

For example, if a problem has a small impact and is unlikely to happen, it may only require monitoring. But if a problem could seriously affect an organization's services and has a higher chance of happening, it may need stronger action.

We learned that organizations have limited money, time, and staff. Because of this, they need to focus first on the risks that could cause the biggest problems.

CISA also explains that organizations should focus on practical and high-priority security actions that can reduce risk and improve protection. Their Cybersecurity Performance Goals are designed so that organizations can choose actions based on their own environment, needs, and level of risk (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency [CISA], n.d.).

This idea was similar to what we did during our fieldwork. We did not recommend the same solution for every problem. We considered the level of risk and what would be realistic for the organization.

Existing Security Controls Are Important

Another lesson from our fieldwork was that we should not only focus on what the organization is doing wrong.

Before the assessment, I thought our main task would be to find weaknesses. But we also had to identify the controls that were already being used.

We noticed that even simple actions can help reduce risk.

Examples include limiting access to certain areas, keeping documents in proper storage, checking equipment, monitoring important supplies, assigning responsibilities to workers, performing regular maintenance, and following proper procedures.

These controls may seem simple, but they can make a big difference.

This taught me that a security control does not always have to be expensive or highly technical.

Sometimes, something as simple as regularly checking equipment or properly storing documents can prevent a serious problem.

CISA also recommends practical security measures that organizations can realistically apply instead of assuming that every organization has a large budget or a full cybersecurity team (CISA, n.d.).

This was important for our group because the best recommendation is not always the most advanced one. A recommendation should be useful, realistic, and possible for the organization to continue using.

Risk Cannot Always Be Completely Removed

Before this activity, I thought that once a risk was found, the goal was always to remove it completely.

Our fieldwork taught me that this is not always possible.

Some risks can be reduced, but they cannot be totally removed. There may always be a chance that something could still go wrong.

Because of this, we learned about different ways of treating risk.

For some lower-level risks, the organization may decide to accept the risk. This means that the organization understands the risk but believes that the current controls are already enough or that adding more controls may not be necessary.

For other risks, the organization may choose to mitigate the risk. This means adding or improving controls to reduce the chance of the problem happening or to reduce its impact.

During our assessment, we recommended treatments based on the risks we identified. These recommendations included general improvements such as better monitoring, stronger protection of important areas and resources, continued maintenance, improved organization, clearer procedures, and staff awareness.

We tried to make our recommendations realistic instead of suggesting solutions that would be difficult or too expensive to maintain.

That part of the activity taught me that good risk management is about making practical decisions.

Residual Risk Made More Sense in Practice

Another concept that became clearer during our fieldwork was residual risk.

Residual risk is the risk that remains after controls have already been applied.

At first, this term sounded complicated to me. But during our assessment, it became easier to understand.

For example, an organization can improve its controls and lower the chance of a problem happening, but the risk may still not become zero.

This showed us that security controls are meant to reduce risk, not guarantee that nothing bad will ever happen.

After applying or recommending additional controls, some of the risks in our assessment became lower. This showed us how useful security controls can be.

However, we also realized that some situations are partly outside the organization's control. Problems may come from outside suppliers, unexpected equipment failures, natural events, human mistakes, or other conditions.

Because of this, organizations should continue reviewing risks even after controls are already in place.

People Are an Important Part of Security

One of the strongest lessons from our assessment was the importance of people.

An organization may have good equipment, policies, storage areas, or security tools, but those controls will only work if people use them correctly.

Employees are the ones who handle documents, use equipment, manage supplies, follow procedures, and respond to problems.

This means that human behavior has a big effect on security.

Training, reminders, clear responsibilities, and regular checking are important because people can make mistakes.

At the same time, our fieldwork taught us not to immediately blame workers whenever we see a problem.

Sometimes, an issue may happen because employees have too many responsibilities, there are not enough resources, instructions are unclear, or the current system is difficult to follow.

This changed the way I think about risk assessments.

The goal should not be to find someone to blame. The goal should be to understand why a risk exists and how the organization can improve.

I think this is one of the most important lessons we learned because information security depends on cooperation between people.

Confidentiality During and After the Assessment

We also realized that the risk assessment itself contains information that should be protected.

A risk assessment may include details about important assets, current controls, problems, weaknesses, and recommendations.

If all of these details were shared publicly, someone could possibly use the information in a harmful way.

Because of this, we need to be careful when writing about our experience.

That is why this reflection only discusses the general findings and lessons from our assessment. It does not reveal the organization's name, specific workers, exact locations, or detailed vulnerabilities.

For me, this is also an example of practicing confidentiality.

It would not make sense to study information security while carelessly sharing sensitive information from the organization we assessed.

What I Learned From the Experience

Overall, our fieldwork helped me understand information security better than simply reading about it.

I learned that security is not only about stopping cyberattacks. It is about protecting information, people, equipment, resources, and services.

I also learned that risk assessment requires careful thinking.

We cannot simply look at a problem and immediately say that it is high risk. We have to consider its possible impact, how likely it is to happen, what controls are already in place, and what additional action may be needed.

Another important lesson was that simple security measures can still be effective.

Proper storage, access control, regular checking, maintenance, monitoring, planning, and staff awareness may sound basic, but they can prevent many problems.

Our fieldwork also taught us that no organization can completely remove every possible risk.

Instead, organizations need to understand their risks, reduce them when necessary, accept some risks when reasonable, and continue checking whether their controls are working.

ISO/IEC 27001 also supports this type of risk-based approach. It explains that organizations should manage information security risks in a structured way so that confidentiality, integrity, and availability can be protected (ISO, 2022).

In the end, this experience helped connect our classroom lessons to the real world.

Terms such as assets, threats, risks, controls, impact, probability, mitigation, acceptance, and residual risk became easier to understand because we were able to apply them during an actual assessment.

More importantly, I learned that information security is everyone's responsibility.

It does not belong only to an IT department. Employees, managers, staff members, and other people in the organization all have a role in protecting information and resources.

The biggest lesson I learned from our risk assessment fieldwork is that good security does not mean removing every risk completely. It means knowing what needs to be protected, understanding what could go wrong, using practical controls, and continuing to improve when needed.

Our fieldwork showed me that information security is not just something we study for an exam. It is something organizations practice every day in order to protect their people, information, resources, and services.
