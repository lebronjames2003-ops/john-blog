Becoming a SOC Pilot: Why Autonomous Security Still Needs Human Judgment

Evaluating where agentic AI can act independently, why asset context matters, and the skills needed to supervise an autonomous SOC.

By John • October 16, 2026 • 9-minute read
🛡️

Automate with purpose.
Keep people accountable.

A responsible SOC pilot knows when to trust automation and when to take control.

A security operations center, or SOC, helps an organization detect, investigate, and respond to security threats. The idea of allowing artificial intelligence to perform much of this work is attractive. If machines handle repetitive investigations, analysts could spend more time understanding serious incidents and improving defenses. However, a fast response is only useful when it is also appropriate. An automated action that stops an attack but unnecessarily interrupts an essential service can create another crisis.

After reading John Velisaris’s IBM article, I believe the SOC pilot model is realistic as a system of limited, supervised autonomy. It becomes irresponsible when organizations interpret it as permission to remove meaningful human oversight. The important question is how much authority an AI agent should receive, given the evidence available and the consequences of being wrong.

Understanding the SOC Pilot Model

Velisaris (2025) compares future SOC analysts with airline pilots: automation would handle standard operations while humans take command during uncertainty. He also explains that automated responses depend on context. Whether an affected device is a critical server or a workstation can change the appropriate action. His proposed multi-agent approach gathers information from other security and asset-management systems to support decisions. However, he acknowledges that the full transition to autonomous SOCs remains unfinished.

I find the comparison useful because it changes the analyst’s role from processing every alert to supervising an operational system. Still, I would be careful about carrying the analogy too far. My concern is that a SOC must handle intentional deception alongside technical failures. An attacker may deliberately create activity that resembles normal behavior or introduce misleading information into an investigation.

The model therefore needs more than an analyst who appears after something obviously unfamiliar happens. It needs people who define safe operating boundaries, monitor performance, and challenge apparently ordinary decisions. A familiar-looking alert can still produce an unfamiliar consequence.

Where AI Could Act Independently

My starting point would be to grant independence according to the possible harm of an action. I would allow more autonomy for narrow, well-tested tasks whose effects are limited and easy to inspect.

For example, an agent could collect relevant logs, group duplicate alerts, retrieve approved threat information, and build a timeline. It could create a case containing the original evidence and a summary of what remains uncertain. These activities could reduce repetitive work without immediately changing production systems.

Even evidence collection needs restrictions. The agent should use approved sources and avoid sending confidential logs to unauthorized services. Its summary should link to the underlying records so an analyst can check whether the explanation matches the evidence. A convincing paragraph should never become a substitute for a verifiable investigation.

I would also consider automatic containment for carefully defined cases. An organization might authorize temporary isolation of an ordinary employee endpoint when multiple reliable signals indicate malicious activity, the device’s identity is verified, and policy permits the action. That authorization should exclude protected assets and include a clear recovery procedure.

Reversibility helps, but it is not enough. A device may be reconnectable while the missed business activity cannot be recovered. Before authorizing isolation, the organization should consider who relies on that device and how quickly support can restore service.

Where Human Judgment Must Remain

I would require human approval for actions with substantial service, safety, financial, or evidentiary consequences. Examples include shutting down shared infrastructure, disabling a widely used service account, deleting files, or rebuilding systems that may contain important evidence.

Consider a hypothetical alert involving a server that supports clinical operations. Disconnecting it might limit malicious activity, but it might also interrupt essential work. A responsible decision would require input from the incident team and the service owner, including the availability of alternatives. The agent could gather evidence and propose options, while authorized people decide which consequences are acceptable.

Human responsibility should also remain with decisions about public statements, affected customers, and organizational risk acceptance. An agent can draft a report, but people must verify its claims and approve communications. Management should remain accountable for the authority it delegates.

That does not mean every emergency action must wait for a meeting. Organizations could approve narrowly defined emergency responses in advance. For example, a validated containment action might execute immediately within a specified scope while notifying the on-call analyst. The key is that its authority comes from an approved policy with tested limits.

A zero-day attack would be one reason to escalate, but not the only reason. Conflicting evidence, uncertain asset identity, unusual dependencies, or repeated tool failures should also trigger human involvement. An AI agent should not have to recognize the exact attack before recognizing that it lacks enough information to act safely.

When Asset Information Is Missing or Wrong

The strongest lesson I take from the IBM article is that asset knowledge is part of response capability. A detection may correctly identify suspicious activity while the response fails because the organization misunderstands the affected system.

Imagine a server that is incorrectly recorded as a normal workstation. An agent might apply a workstation containment rule and interrupt a shared service. The reverse error could also matter: treating an ordinary endpoint as protected infrastructure might delay an otherwise appropriate response.

Missing information should therefore remain visibly unknown. I would not allow an agent to interpret an empty criticality field as evidence that an asset has low importance. Similarly, a hostname suggesting a workstation should be treated as a clue rather than confirmation of its business role.

I would require asset information to include ownership, function, criticality, service dependencies, source, and last verification date. Different questions may need different authoritative sources. A device-management tool might establish operating-system details, while a service owner confirms the impact of disconnecting the device.

Multiple agents would not automatically solve this problem. If several agents consult systems copied from the same outdated inventory, their agreement may repeat one error. Independent corroboration matters more than the number of agents producing the same answer.

When important context is unavailable, I would let the agent continue approved evidence collection, mark the uncertainty, and escalate the containment decision. Where delay itself creates serious danger, an organization should have a specific emergency policy rather than letting the model invent its own exception.

Responsible Autonomy Requires More Than Confidence

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework identifies reliability, safety, accountability, transparency, and other characteristics of trustworthy AI. It also calls for clear human–AI oversight responsibilities and mechanisms to override or deactivate systems whose behavior conflicts with their intended use (Tabassi, 2023). These principles support my view that autonomy should remain conditional.

I would implement those principles through technical limits outside the agent’s own reasoning. An enforcement layer should check whether the requested action is permitted for the target asset. The agent should receive only the permissions needed for its tasks. Limits on the number of affected systems could prevent one faulty decision from becoming a widespread outage.

I would also treat content gathered during an investigation as untrusted evidence. A suspicious message or log entry might contain instructions aimed at influencing an AI assistant. My proposed boundary is that evidence can inform an investigation but cannot grant permissions or change response policy. An instruction found inside an alert should never override the organization’s rules.

Every action should leave an audit trail: the evidence used, asset context, applicable policy, tool request, outcome, and any human approval. Recording only the AI’s explanation would be insufficient because an explanation can sound reasonable even when its inputs are wrong.

Deployment should begin with observation and recommendations before progressing to restricted execution. I would test ordinary cases alongside stale inventories, conflicting data, unavailable tools, and incorrect classifications. The purpose would be to discover whether the system stops or escalates appropriately when its assumptions fail.

The Human Must Be Able to Take Over

There is a practical weakness in saying that humans will intervene only when needed: they must notice the need and understand the situation quickly enough.

I would worry about a SOC pilot who receives a complicated incident after remaining outside the investigation for hours. To make takeover meaningful, the system should present a concise timeline, original evidence, completed actions, unresolved questions, and suggested alternatives. The analyst should also have authority to pause the agent and access the necessary response tools.

Oversight would require staffing, training, and exercises. If every escalation reaches an unavailable person, the presence of a human approval step offers little protection. Regular simulations could help analysts practice taking control after an incorrect automated action.

I would judge success using more than the number of closed alerts. Useful measures would include missed incidents, incorrect containment, service disruption, escalation quality, and the time required for a human to understand and assume control. These measures would help reveal whether apparent efficiency is hiding operational harm.

Preparing Myself to Become a SOC Pilot

As a student, I see this model as a reason to strengthen my technical foundations. I would need networking, operating-system, identity-management, and log-analysis skills to understand what an agent reports. Without those foundations, I might accept a polished explanation that the evidence does not support.

I would also need practical incident-response and scripting skills. Understanding APIs and automation would help me inspect workflows, test their limits, and build safer responses. Asset management would be equally important because effective action depends on knowing what a system does and who relies on it.

Another skill would be evaluating AI critically. I would need to recognize uncertainty, question unsupported conclusions, and test whether an agent handles missing or misleading information appropriately. Communication would help me explain response options to service owners and management without hiding uncertainty behind technical language.

My goal would be to become someone who can verify, challenge, and improve automation. The realistic SOC pilot model gives AI a useful operational role while keeping people responsible for its boundaries and consequences. To earn that role, I would need to understand both the security incident and the organization affected by the response.
